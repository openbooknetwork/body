In the 1850s the French neurologist Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne stimulated single muscles of the face with electric current through two electrodes on the skin. His main subject was an old shoemaker whose face had little feeling, so the current did not hurt him. Duchenne had the expressions photographed and published them in 1862 as Mécanisme de la physionomie humaine.

He found that the [zygomaticus major](@zygomaticus-major) alone pulls the mouth into a smile that looks false. A smile of real joy also tightens the [orbicularis oculi](@orbicularis-oculi) around the eye, which raises the cheek and creases the skin at the outer corner. Few people can tighten that part of the muscle at will. Psychologists now call the full smile the Duchenne smile.

Charles Darwin used several of Duchenne's photographs in The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals in 1872.
