When the [biceps](@biceps-brachii) and the [brachialis](@brachialis) bend the elbow, their filaments keep their length and slide past each other. Heads that stick out from each myosin filament grab the actin beside them, tilt and pull it toward the middle of the sarcomere, let go and grab again. Each stroke moves the actin 5 to 10 nanometres.

@image sliding-filament-openstax-f67064

The signal to start is calcium. A nerve impulse spreads over the fibre and into it along fine tubes, and a store inside the fibre, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, releases calcium. Calcium moves aside troponin and tropomyosin, the proteins that cover the binding sites on actin. When the impulses stop, pumps take the calcium back and the muscle relaxes.

Every stroke uses one molecule of ATP, and the head needs ATP to let go of the actin. After death the ATP runs out, the heads stay attached and the muscles stiffen. This rigor mortis lasts until the muscle proteins break down, some 15 to 25 hours later.
