A contracting muscle runs on ATP, but a fibre holds only enough for about 3 seconds of hard work. Phosphocreatine stored in the fibre rebuilds ATP, and the two together last 8 to 10 seconds, almost enough for a 100 metre sprint. Breaking down glycogen without oxygen gives about a minute and a half more and makes lactic acid.

For longer work the fibres burn glycogen, fat and other fuels with oxygen in their mitochondria, which can go on for hours. In a long run the [quadriceps](@quadriceps), [hamstrings](@hamstrings), [gluteus maximus](@gluteus-maximus), [gastrocnemius](@gastrocnemius) and [soleus](@soleus) do most of the work and use most of the oxygen.

Less than a quarter of the energy a muscle uses becomes movement. The rest becomes heat, which is why exercise warms the body. Fatigue sets in as glycogen runs low and the products of the work build up. A cramp is a painful contraction that goes on by itself, most often in the calf, and stretching the muscle usually eases it.
