Two deep grooves divide each hemisphere into four lobes, named after the skull bones above them. The central sulcus separates the [frontal lobe](@left-frontal-lobe) in front from the [parietal lobe](@left-parietal-lobe) behind it. The lateral sulcus runs back along the side and separates both from the [temporal lobe](@left-temporal-lobe) below. The [occipital lobe](@left-occipital-lobe) forms the back.

The frontal lobe plans and starts movement and holds back impulses. The parietal lobe receives touch and maps the body in space. The temporal lobe hears and stores memories, and the occipital lobe sees. A fifth lobe, the insula, lies folded inside the lateral sulcus.

In 1848 an iron rod 1.1 metres long shot through the front of the brain of Phineas Gage, a railway foreman in Vermont. He survived and could walk, talk and remember, but his doctor reported that his friends found him "no longer Gage": impatient, rude and unable to keep to a plan.
