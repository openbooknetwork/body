In 1873 Camillo Golgi, a doctor at a hospital near Milan, hardened brain tissue in potassium dichromate and soaked it in silver nitrate. A few nerve cells in every hundred turned black down to their finest branches, while the rest stayed clear. For the first time a whole neuron could be seen. Golgi thought the fibres fused into one net.

From 1888 Santiago Ramón y Cajal, in Barcelona, used the stain on the [cerebellum](@cerebellum) and the [cortex](@cerebral-cortex) of young animals and drew what he saw. Each neuron ended free, close to the next one without joining it, and signals ran one way, from the dendrites through the cell body to the axon.

The two shared the Nobel Prize in 1906, and in his lecture Golgi still defended his net. The electron microscope settled the question in the 1950s by showing a gap of a few hundredths of a micrometre at every synapse.
