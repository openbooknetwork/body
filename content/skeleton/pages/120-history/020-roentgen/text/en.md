On 8 November 1895 in Würzburg, the physicist Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen saw a screen glow in his darkened laboratory while he passed a current through a glass tube wrapped in black card. Rays from the tube went through card, paper and wood. He called them X-rays, X for unknown.

@image roentgen-portrait-8c64d2

On 22 December he photographed the [left hand](@left-hand-bones) of his wife, Anna Bertha, in an exposure of about 15 minutes. The bones and her ring showed clearly, the flesh only faintly. He sent his paper, On a new kind of rays, to the Würzburg Physical-Medical Society at the end of December, and the picture went round the world within weeks.

@image first-x-ray-hand-a8af6e

Bone shows because its calcium absorbs X-rays far more than soft tissue does. Within months doctors used the rays to find broken bones and bullets. In 1901 Röntgen received the first Nobel Prize in Physics.
