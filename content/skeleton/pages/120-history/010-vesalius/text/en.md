In 1543 Andreas Vesalius, a 28-year-old professor of anatomy at Padua, published De humani corporis fabrica, On the fabric of the human body, in Basel. Its woodcuts showed the body in more detail and more correctly than any earlier book. The first of its seven books covers the bones.

@image vesalius-portrait-d1c7ec

Anatomy until then rested on Galen, a Greek physician of the second century who had dissected animals. Vesalius dissected human bodies himself and found where Galen's account did not fit them. In humans the [mandible](@mandible) is one bone, not two, and the [sternum](@sternum) has three parts, not seven.

Three full-page woodcuts show the skeleton from the front, the side and the back. In the side view the skeleton leans on a tomb, one hand resting on a skull, as if in thought.
