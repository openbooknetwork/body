Lymph vessels begin as closed tubes among the cells, a little wider than blood capillaries. Their wall is a single layer of thin cells that overlap at the edges. Fluid pressing from outside pushes an edge inwards like a flap and enters. Pressure from inside presses the edges together, so lymph cannot leak back out.

Fine threads anchor the wall to the tissue around it. When the tissue swells, they pull the tube open and drainage speeds up. Once inside, the fluid is called lymph. It is the same fluid that bathes the tissues, and it carries water, salts, some protein and anything the tissue has shed, such as bacteria or cancer cells.

In the skin of the leg these tubes join into vessels that climb beside the superficial veins, such as the [great saphenous vein](@great-saphenous-veins), to the [nodes of the groin](@inguinal-nodes).
