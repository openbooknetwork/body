The [thymus](@thymus) lies behind the upper breastbone, in front of the heart and the great vessels, in two lobes. In a child it is large: about 15 g at birth and 30 to 40 g at puberty. After that it slowly turns to fat, and in old age only a few grams of working tissue remain inside a fatty remnant.

Its job was unknown until 1961, when Jacques Miller removed it from newborn mice. They grew up short of lymphocytes, wasted away, and accepted skin grafts from other strains that normal mice rejected. The thymus is where one kind of lymphocyte matures, and those cells are named after it: T cells.
