For some 1,400 years, medicine in Europe and the Islamic world followed Galen of Pergamon, a Greek physician who worked in Rome in the second century. He taught that the [liver](@liver) turned food into blood, and that the veins carried this blood out to the body, where it was used up as nourishment.

@image galen-7bc36f

Some of the blood, he held, seeped from the [right ventricle](@right-ventricle) into the [left](@left-ventricle) through invisible pores in the wall between them. There it mixed with air from the lungs and left by the arteries as a warmer, brighter blood. Later anatomists found no such pores in the wall.

Galen did show that arteries carry blood. Earlier Greek anatomists had thought they carried air, since the arteries of a corpse are nearly empty. Galen tied off a stretch of artery in a living animal, opened it and found it full of blood.
