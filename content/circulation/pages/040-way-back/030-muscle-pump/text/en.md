At the end of the capillaries little of the heart's push is left, about 10 millimetres of mercury. Yet blood from the feet has to climb more than a metre to the heart. In the legs the deep veins, the [posterior tibial](@posterior-tibial-veins), [popliteal](@popliteal-veins) and [femoral veins](@femoral-veins), lie between the muscles.

When the [calf muscles](@calf-muscles) contract, they squeeze these veins and push the blood upwards. Valves every few centimetres close behind it, so it cannot fall back when the muscles relax. Each step pumps the veins of the leg partly empty.

Standing still, the pressure in the veins of the feet rises to about 90 millimetres of mercury, the weight of the blood column up to the heart. Walking brings it below about 25. A soldier who stands at attention for long enough can faint, because blood pools in the legs and too little returns to the heart.
