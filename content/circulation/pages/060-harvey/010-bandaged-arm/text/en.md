Before Harvey the blood was thought to ebb and flow in the veins and to be used up by the body as it went. Harvey tied a bandage round a man's upper arm tight enough to stop the veins but not the arteries. The veins of the forearm, the [cephalic](@cephalic-veins) on the thumb side, the [basilic](@basilic-veins) on the little finger side and the [median cubital](@median-cubital-veins) that joins them at the elbow, swelled below the bandage, with knots at the valves. Pressing the blood out of a length of vein towards the hand, the length stayed empty: the valves let blood pass only towards the heart.

He then reckoned the output. The heart holds about two ounces and beats some 72 times a minute, so in half an hour it sends out more blood than the whole body holds, more than any food could supply. The blood must go round. De motu cordis was printed in Frankfurt in 1628, 72 pages long.
